# Composition modes

Choose a mode from source condition first. The modes are different image–type relationships, not color swaps.

## Read the source state

- **Complete scene** — the photo already has meaningful environment, action, and usable negative space. Preserve it; do not rebuild a new background merely to make a collage.
- **Cutout-ready subject** — the person, animal, or object has a clear silhouette while the original background is weak or irrelevant. Reconstruct around the subject.
- **Photo-block breakout** — the photo is worth keeping as a rectangle, but a face, hair, hand, object, or animal can cross its edge to create depth.

## 1. Original-scene annotation

Use for a complete scene, interaction, or atmospheric photo.

- Keep the original image full bleed or almost full bleed.
- Use one large title group that follows existing negative space, plus optional microcopy or one small semantic cue.
- Treat the photograph itself as the background field. Use its sky, wall, architecture, foliage, or ground as the typography path; do not add a color field just to signal “collage.”
- Let a meaningful foreground object interrupt a word only when it strengthens the scene's real scale or action.
- Keep wording as one visual group even when split into several pieces: same paper, same type system, and a clear reading path through the scene.
- Let final exact type sit on top; do not place it in a new card around the photo.
- Best for: daily life, pets, events, travel, a strong original setting.

## 2. Pure-field subject reconstruction

Use for a cutout-ready subject or when the user wants a bold poster rather than the original setting.

- Build one full-canvas paper or color field from source-derived colors.
- Put the cutout subject in front of the field; use a clean outline only when it reads as cut paper, not a generic sticker.
- When the person, pet, or object is meaningfully tied to an environmental anchor, retain that full anchor as a single cutout group. For example, keep a hut's roof, wall, and door together for a person standing at its door; never leave only a floating roof or other semantic fragment.
- Make oversized display type the second major shape. It may sit behind the subject, on top, or split around it according to the chosen interaction.
- Use only one supporting material family, preferably a source detail or a thematic counterpart.
- Best for: portrait, pet, speaking/performance, playful object shot.

## 3. Photo-block with selective breakout

Use when the photograph should remain visibly photographic but needs stronger collage depth.

- Keep one raw or torn photo rectangle as a real material.
- Let only a meaningful part of the subject escape: hair, a hand, an animal head, a microphone, a bow, or a held object.
- Put the required title on the topmost layer across the pure field, photo block, and non-critical breakout area.
- Use one strong field and at most one graphic counterweight. Do not add matching text panels.
- Best for: portrait, fashion, event, toys, illustrated subjects.

## 4. Type-led editorial scaffold

Use when the phrase itself is the strongest visual material.

- Make one or two oversized words form the canvas skeleton, often cropped at an edge.
- Insert the photo, cutout, or small detail images through gaps in the type rather than arranging them as a grid.
- Allow the subject to overlap the large words when it creates a clear foreground event.
- Use a display family for the scaffold and one restrained support role; do not add a third competing headline.
- Best for: a short slogan, issue-cover feeling, month/event recaps, strong named themes.

## 5. Image–type equal-weight interlock

Use when picture and words must feel equally important.

- Let the photo become a window, crop, or object occupying roughly 35–60% of the canvas.
- Let the display title occupy a comparable visual weight and cross at least one photo edge.
- Use the background field to connect them, not to divide them into columns.
- Keep the title topmost unless the reference explicitly demands a behind-subject letter.
- Best for: one-photo stories, editorial social graphics, invitations, campaigns.

## Vary directions honestly

When three directions are requested, choose modes from different families. A useful set is:

1. original-scene annotation;
2. pure-field reconstruction or photo-block breakout;
3. type-led scaffold or equal-weight interlock.

Reject variants that only move the title while preserving the same source state, z-order, and photo treatment.

## Mode quality gate

Before delivery, verify:

1. Did the chosen mode follow the source state rather than a preferred template?
2. Does the image–type relationship visibly match the mode?
3. Is the final required type in its planned layer, especially when it must be topmost?
4. Are all added materials either source-derived or semantically specific?
5. If a scene was separated, does the retained anchor still explain the subject's action and scale? If not, restore the missing source context.
6. Could a viewer mistake the composition for a generic card, dashboard, or social-media template? If yes, rebuild it.
