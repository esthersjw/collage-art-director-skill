# Typography and quality control

## Typography roles

Use no more than three roles:

1. **Display** — one expressive title or dominant word.
2. **Support** — a short phrase, subtitle, or second level.
3. **Microcopy** — date, place, caption, edition, or small aside.

Use one coherent family or a deliberate two-family contrast. Do not make every word a different font.

## Typeface direction

Choose a real installed or loadable font. Verify availability before promising exact output.

Useful directions:

- modern editorial sans: condensed or grotesk display plus neutral sans;
- refined serif: high-contrast serif plus small grotesk;
- humanist handwritten: irregular but legible lettering for one role only;
- photocopy/DIY: bold utilitarian sans or mono with print texture applied around, not inside, glyphs;
- playful cut-paper: one display family with manually varied size or baseline, keeping letterforms real.

For Chinese, prioritize fonts with complete Chinese glyph coverage. A Latin display face may pair with a Chinese sans, serif, or handwriting face, but their stroke weight and visual temperature must agree.

## Exact-text rules

- Preserve spelling, punctuation, capitalization, and language.
- Choose line breaks semantically.
- Do not let a line end with an orphaned function word when avoidable.
- Do not allow AI-generated pseudo-letters in final required copy.
- Keep body or support text horizontally readable unless the concept explicitly requires rotation.

## Model-led display exception

Use model-rendered lettering only as an explicitly chosen display treatment, never as the default typesetting route. Retain it only when a single short phrase is exact, legible at phone-preview scale, and visibly more integrated with the source medium than a real-font overlay.

Always render Chinese, names, dates, URLs, brand copy, support copy, and microcopy with real fonts. If the model phrase is even slightly misspelled, ambiguous, generic, or hidden by the subject, replace it with deterministic type rather than attempting to repair isolated letters.

## Placement rules

- Measure contrast where the glyphs overlap the image, not against the average palette.
- Do not cover eyes, mouths, hands, pet faces, or the key object unless the overlap is intentionally conceptual and still readable.
- Let large type crop at a canvas edge only when enough letterform remains identifiable.
- When type overlaps a cutout, decide which specific letters go behind and which remain in front.
- Keep label fragments separated by 2–5% of canvas width or height.
- Treat negative space as an active material, not an area that must be filled.

## “AI-looking” warning signs

Revise when several appear:

- mathematically centered everything;
- identical margins and repeated card sizes;
- one generic rounded sans for all roles;
- random capitalization or malformed letters;
- uniform sticker outlines around every object;
- arbitrary gradients, glow, blur, fog, or depth-of-field;
- decorative stars, arrows, tape, and grain unrelated to the source;
- text placed only in obvious empty space;
- three concepts that share the same skeleton;
- decorations generated more sharply or realistically than the source photo;
- fake sauce, food, logos, accessories, or scenery;
- every element competing at equal scale.

## Final review

Ask:

1. What is the first thing the eye sees?
2. What is the second?
3. Why is each decoration present?
4. Does the color come from the source?
5. Is the type part of the composition rather than a caption?
6. Is the subject still recognizably the user’s subject?
7. Could any element be removed without losing the idea?
8. Are the three directions structurally different?
9. Is all required text exact and readable?
10. Does the result feel designed rather than automatically arranged?

Remove any element without a clear answer.
