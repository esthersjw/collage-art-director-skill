# Reference interaction grammar

Use this file when the user supplies collage references. Read the references as a system of visual relationships, not as a bag of colors or stickers.

## The four-layer read

For each reference, identify:

1. **Background field** — a full-canvas color, paper, or scene that establishes the image's temperature.
2. **Photo object** — a full-bleed scene, raw rectangle, rounded window, crop, or small insert; it is not automatically a centered card.
3. **Type mass** — words act as large visual shapes. They may cross the image edge, sit on the photo, break into pieces, or become the layout grid.
4. **Graphic material** — a star, arrow, strip, silhouette, or label is allowed only when it creates direction, interruption, or counterweight.

Keep every layer active. Do not isolate photo and wording into polite columns or matching cards.

## Six interaction families

Choose one primary family. Combine only when the composition needs it.

### 1. Type crosses a photo rectangle

Use a strong flat background and a photographic rectangle or crop. Let a short, oversized title sit partly on the image and partly on the background. The photo and word should share the same spatial event; neither is a caption.

### 2. Full-bleed photo with expressive type overlay

Use the whole photograph as the ground. Let large, deliberately imperfect display lettering travel through the scene and crop at edges. Preserve important faces and eyes; text may pass over architecture, foliage, clothes, or other non-critical detail.

### 3. Image window with satellite materials

Place one rounded or raw image window within a confident color field. Use a few small labels inside or near the image. Add one or two large flat graphic forms only when they push the eye between image and field; do not scatter decorative stickers evenly.

### 4. Type-led field with photo inserts

Make oversized words the main scaffold. Let small photographs pass across, interrupt, or nest between word shapes. Use a dominant background field and asymmetric positioning; the photos should feel collected, not tiled.

### 5. Label strips interrupt a full action photo

Use an energetic full-bleed photograph. Put meaningful word fragments on uneven paper strips that cross the action. Let labels cover non-critical portions of people or objects when that interruption is the point, but never cover eyes, mouths, or key gesture information.

### 6. Word-and-photo interlock

Split a large phrase across the canvas. Put one photo rectangle through a word gap, beside a letter stem, or over a cropped word edge. Add microcopy only after the large interlock reads clearly.

## Build rules

- Let the background field occupy a meaningful part of the canvas; it is not merely empty space around a photo.
- Use a raw crop, rounded window, or insert intentionally. Do not turn every photo into the same framed rectangle.
- Make one visible image–type collision, overlap, nesting, or interruption.
- Use no more than two material families. A large star, arrow, strip, or silhouette must have a compositional job, not just a decorative one.
- Prefer uneven, tactile print texture and purposeful edge crops over glossy polish, UI cards, or evenly spaced modules.
- Keep final required copy as real typography. Translate expressive reference lettering into a font, letter spacing, outline, shadow, or manually placed glyphs; never delegate exact copy to the image model.

## Reference-driven quality gate

Before delivery, ask:

1. Could the photo and words be cleanly separated into columns or cards? If yes, rebuild the relationship.
2. Does the background field actively connect the photo and type?
3. Can the viewer name the chosen interaction family from the finished image?
4. Does every accent redirect attention or create tension between the photo and type?
5. Did the design avoid generic business-poster, dashboard, beauty-ad, and template-card signals?
